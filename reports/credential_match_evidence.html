What public data can and cannot tell you about a credential

Two trade credentials, two metros, run end to end through federal sources. The point is not the answers — it is which parts of the question the standard data can actually answer, and where it will quietly mislead you.

Aaron Gordon Sources NCES/BLS CIP-SOC 2020 · BLS OEWS May 2025 · CPS ASEC 2018–2025 Reproducible one command

Two findings

36%

of the jobs a credential points to are invisible locally

For Machine Tool Technology in Cleveland, only 3 of 13 crosswalk matches appear in local BLS wage data. The missing ones are not absent jobs — they are suppressed cells, and nothing in the data marks the difference.

90%

of priced moves out of that job go down in wage

Where Cleveland machinists actually move next, 5 of the 6 destinations with a local wage pay less than the origin. Placement and progression are different questions, and only one of them is usually measured.

Case 1 — Machine Tool Technology, Cleveland

The part that matters

The 10 unpublished matches account for 289,330 of the 814,580 US jobs this credential points to — 36%. The largest, 51-4081, has 124,590 jobs nationally and nothing published in Cleveland, a manufacturing metro. Those jobs exist; OEWS suppresses small cells for confidentiality and gives you no way to separate suppression from genuine absence.

A matching engine that reads a missing cell as “no demand here” will be wrong most often in smaller metros — which is where a large share of trade schools are.

Five of the six destinations with a local wage and at least three observed movers pay less than machinists do in Cleveland. The one that pays more is water and wastewater treatment, at +9%.

Worth noting separately: the second-largest destination is welding, and the tenth is CNC operation — genuine trade-to-trade moves. Both are reported by CPS as broad groups rather than detailed occupations, so neither carries a local wage. The moves are real; the pay is simply not measurable at that resolution.

Why this is a separate question

The crosswalk describes entry — which jobs a program prepares you for. It says nothing about progression, and that is a gap in scope rather than an error. It matters because WIOA grades states on employment in the fourth quarter after exit, not on placement. A match built on the crosswalk alone is optimising the first job; the scorecard is graded on the second.

Method and limits

Credential map
NCES/BLS CIP-SOC crosswalk, 2020 CIP to 2018 SOC. Expert-assigned, not empirical, by its own documentation.
Local demand & pay
BLS OEWS, May 2025 reference period, metropolitan area series via the public API. Reference period, not publication date.
Progression
Observed year-over-year occupation changes, CPS ASEC 2018–2025 via IPUMS, survey-weighted.
Sample
67 observed moves for machinists, 23 for electricians. CPS is thin at occupation level — these are directional, not precise, and the movers column is shown on every row so the reader can discount accordingly.
Broad groups
CPS reports some occupations as aggregates rather than detailed SOC. No local wage exists for an aggregate, so those rows carry no pay figure rather than a guessed one.
Suppression
“Suppressed?” marks an occupation large nationally but absent locally. OEWS cannot confirm suppression, which is exactly the problem — the label is an inference, and the honest answer is that this source cannot tell you.
Not claimed
Nothing here predicts what an individual will do. It describes the opportunity structure around an occupation in a place, from public data, with the derivation visible.
Two credentials, two metros. The same run works for any CIP program and any of 17 metros currently loaded; extending the metro list is a data-entry task, not a modelling one.
